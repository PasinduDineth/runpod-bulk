THE ADVENTURE OF THE NINTH HOUR
CHAPTER I: THE GENTLEMAN WHO KNEW THE HOUR
WATSON — NARRATION AND DIALOGUE

[Narration | Voice Description: Reflective, intimate, and faintly unsettled. Begin with the measured calm of a doctor recalling an old case; let the memory of the ninth chime carry quiet unease. Until the ninth of November, I had regarded a clock as an innocent instrument. It may hurry a man to his train; it does not, as a rule, threaten his life. I have since found it difficult to hear nine strokes without remembering Edward Whitmore.

[Narration | Voice Description: Set a rainy, close atmosphere with steady, literary pacing. Sound comfortable in the familiar sitting room, then note Holmes's unusual silence with restrained suspense.]
That afternoon, rain blurred the tall windows of our sitting room at 221B Baker Street. A tired fire flickered beneath the mantel, while I sat reading a medical journal. Outside, carriage wheels hissed over wet stones, and coal smoke drifted through the window frame. On our small table lay a tobacco pouch, cold tea, and several unopened letters which had failed to interest my companion. Sherlock Holmes occupied his customary chair beside the hearth, his fingertips joined beneath his chin. He had scarcely spoken for an hour.

Then the doorbell rang. Holmes raised his eyes, and I saw the bright, mischievous expression that meant some new problem had seized his imagination. Turning towards me, he asked:

Holmes smiled towards the door. Footsteps sounded on the stairs, and a moment later Mrs. Hudson, our elderly landlady, entered the sitting room. She paused on the threshold, her expression betraying an alarm she struggled to conceal.

[Narration | Voice Description: Precise and observant, as if recording a patient's condition. Let concern gather as Watson studies the stranger's pallor and strained appearance.]
Holmes rose to stand beside the hearth as Mrs. Hudson returned, guiding a stranger into our sitting room. The man was perhaps fifty-five, tall, well dressed, and ordinarily, I should have judged, accustomed to being obeyed. On this occasion his fine coat hung awkwardly from his shoulders. His gloves were soaked. His face possessed the grey, strained appearance I have seen in patients awaiting a grave diagnosis. He looked from Holmes to me before addressing my friend.

[Dialogue to Whitmore | Voice Description: Calm, professional, and reassuring. Watson asks a simple question to direct the anxious visitor toward the evidence.]
“Have you the letters?”

[Narration | Voice Description: Patiently suspenseful and exact. Describe the letters and Holmes's close attention to their physical details; pause subtly before the final one is read.]
Whitmore produced three envelopes, each folded with the same fastidious neatness. They were of expensive cream paper, without a sender's mark. Holmes spread them across our table, paying particular attention to the edges rather than the words. The first stated that at eleven on Tuesday morning the portrait of Whitmore’s father would be torn from its hook. The second named Robert Keene and foretold a meeting outside Whitmore’s bank. The third contained only two sentences. Holmes lifted it beneath the lamp and read aloud.
